# Day 4 Reflection — August 15

## What happened

The final phase focused on making the project presentable and ensuring that the required submission evidence and documentation were in place.

I worked through the README/system documentation and go-live documentation requirements and reviewed what the MVP actually supports versus what is intentionally outside the scope.

The team also reviewed the audit history and contribution evidence. One important realization was that our development process did not perfectly match the branch-per-person model we initially intended. We often developed together during Google Meet sessions, with one person sharing their screen while other members contributed ideas, debugging help, testing, and implementation input. As a result, the Git history does not capture every person's contribution equally.

The audit trail now includes documentation work such as:

- `755094d` — Create System_documentation.md
- `75ff4c6` — Update README.md
- `7975b48` — Update TEAM_CHARTER.md
- `d94c889` — Update verify-flows.mjs

I also reviewed the assignment requirements against what we had actually produced so that we could identify what was complete, what needed documentation, and what should honestly be reported as a limitation.

## What went well

- I was able to bring the technical work together with the documentation required for submission.
- I now have a much clearer understanding of what the MVP does and what it deliberately does not do.
- I was able to distinguish between actual evidence in GitHub and contributions that happened collaboratively during meetings.
- The final project has a clearer README, system documentation, go-live information, and audit trail.

## What was harder than expected

- The biggest challenge was making the Git history accurately reflect a collaborative process that often happened live during Google Meet sessions.
- Some commit messages did not follow the Charter's agreed naming convention, which meant the audit trail was not as clean as we originally intended.
- I also had to learn that documentation is part of product delivery, not something that can simply be added at the very end without checking it against the actual system.
- Preparing the audit evidence required me to look back at the work and distinguish between what I personally committed and what I contributed to collaboratively.

## Self-assessment

The sprint taught me that technical delivery and team process are closely connected. I started the project primarily thinking about coordination and the product itself, but I ended up learning a lot about Git, debugging, integration, documentation, and auditability.

I would improve the process in a future project by enforcing the branch and commit conventions from the first technical session and making sure each teammate's contribution is captured immediately.

At the same time, I am proud that despite the team's participation challenges and the compressed timeline, we adapted, worked together, and produced a functioning MVP covering the required two support categories.
